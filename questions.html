Divine hiddenness

If God wants us to know Him, why is He so hard to find?

That’s not a trivial objection. A God who wants relationship can seem strangely quiet. Start by separating two questions: whether God has given any evidence, and whether He has given the kind of evidence we think He should.

The resurrection

What evidence do we actually have?

Christianity puts an extraordinary amount of weight on a historical claim: that Jesus died and was seen alive afterward. That claim deserves more than either “the Bible says so” or “dead people stay dead.”

Unanswered prayer

Why do some prayers seem to go unanswered?

Sometimes the answer may be no. Sometimes not yet. Sometimes we may have asked for something that was never promised. And sometimes there is no explanation we can see at all. Christianity does not give us a formula that turns prayer into control. It gives us permission to keep asking, lamenting, trusting, and telling God the truth about His silence.

Belief and birthplace

Why does where you’re born matter so much?

It clearly does matter. Culture shapes what almost everybody finds plausible. Christianity doesn’t get an exemption from that fact. The harder question is whether explaining why we believe something also tells us whether it is true.

Honest unbelief

What if someone honestly searches and still can’t believe?

That question deserves something better than pretending every unbeliever secretly knows Christianity is true. Christians disagree about parts of the answer. What we can ask first is what Jesus actually says about people who seek, doubt and fail.

Hell

Is eternal punishment really just for one finite lifetime?

Christians do not all understand hell in exactly the same way. Eternal conscious punishment, conditional immortality, and other views have all been argued from Scripture. The hard question is not merely how long a life lasted, but what judgment is, what human freedom means, and whether separation from God is imposed, chosen, or both. This is one of the places where Christians should be careful about speaking more confidently than the text allows.

God and judgment

Why would God create someone He knows will end up in hell?

Knowing an outcome is not the same thing as causing it, but that does not make the problem disappear. If God creates a person knowing their final end, His goodness and human freedom both become part of the question. Christian answers usually appeal to real freedom, God’s desire to save, and the claim that existence itself is a gift. None of those points makes the emotional weight of the question trivial.

Creation and the fall

If God knew humanity would fall, why create us anyway?

The Christian claim is that God judged a world containing freedom, love, redemption, and the possibility of rebellion to be worth creating. That is an explanation, not a proof. The objection remains serious because an all-knowing Creator does not discover the consequences afterward. Christianity answers it most strongly not with an abstract theory, but with the claim that God enters the damage Himself and bears its cost.

Old Testament violence

How can a good God command the killing of entire peoples?

These are among the Bible’s hardest texts. Serious readings have to ask what the ancient war language meant, whether some descriptions use conventional hyperbole, what judgment is being claimed, and how these passages fit with Jesus. Context can clarify some things, but it should not be used to make disturbing passages sound harmless. They deserve close reading rather than a slogan.

The resurrection accounts

Why do the resurrection accounts differ?

The Gospels agree on the central claim that Jesus died, was buried, the tomb was found empty, and His followers came to believe He was alive. They differ in chronology, emphasis, which visitors or angels are mentioned, and how appearances are arranged. Some differences can be harmonized; others are less tidy. The historical question is whether those differences undermine the central claim or look like the normal variation of several ancient accounts.

The cross

Why did Jesus have to die? Couldn’t God just forgive us?

Christianity says forgiveness is free to the forgiven, not costless. The New Testament uses several pictures for the cross: sacrifice, reconciliation, victory, ransom, substitution, and covenant. Christians disagree about how those models fit together. The central claim is that God does not simply wave evil away; in Jesus, God absorbs its cost and reconciles people to Himself.

Atonement

How is it justice to punish an innocent Jesus for guilty people?

If the story were simply “God punished an unrelated innocent person instead,” the objection would be devastating. Christianity makes a stranger claim: in Jesus, God Himself bears the cost of reconciliation. That still raises difficult questions about justice and substitution, and Christian traditions answer them differently. But the claim is not that God found a third party to hurt so everyone else could leave.

Jesus and the Father

If Jesus is God, why does He pray to God?

Classical Christianity says Jesus is not the Father. The Son becomes genuinely human while remaining divine, so prayer is not theater: Jesus lives a real human life of dependence, obedience, love, and communion with the Father. The doctrine of the Trinity is complicated precisely because Christianity is claiming both unity and distinction, not that Jesus is talking to Himself.

Jesus and knowledge

If Jesus is God, how can He not know something the Father knows?

The New Testament presents Jesus as both divine and genuinely human. Christians have traditionally understood passages about limited knowledge through that two-natures claim: the Son does not merely appear human but accepts real human limitations. That answer explains the doctrine’s shape, though it does not make the mystery disappear.

Jesus and the Gospels

Why didn’t Jesus write anything Himself?

We do not know. Jesus taught publicly in a largely oral culture, and His followers preserved and transmitted His teaching before the Gospels were written. That means Christianity depends on testimony about Jesus rather than a book authored by Jesus. Whether that testimony is trustworthy is therefore a historical question, not one Christians should dodge.

Bible and slavery

Why doesn’t the Bible clearly condemn slavery?

The Bible regulates forms of slavery rather than giving the simple universal prohibition a modern reader may expect. The ancient institution was not identical to modern race-based chattel slavery, but that distinction does not remove the moral problem. Christians have argued both for slavery and against it from Scripture. Any serious answer has to reckon with the texts themselves and with the larger biblical claims about human dignity, neighbor-love, liberation, and equality in Christ.

Freedom and foreknowledge

If God already knows every choice I’ll make, am I really free?

Knowing a choice and causing a choice are different things, but the philosophical problem is harder than that sentence makes it sound. Christians have proposed several models of divine foreknowledge and human freedom. The key issue is whether a future act can still be genuinely yours if it is perfectly known in advance. There is no single explanation accepted by every Christian tradition.

God and goodness

Is something good because God commands it—or does God command it because it’s good?

A common Christian answer rejects both horns of the dilemma: goodness is grounded in God’s character, so God neither invents morality arbitrarily nor answers to a moral standard above Himself. That answer then inherits a further question: how do we know that the character attributed to God really is good? That is where abstract philosophy runs into the biblical portrait of God and, especially, Jesus.

Christian disagreement

If the Holy Spirit guides Christians, why do sincere Christians disagree so much?

Christians claim guidance, not infallibility. People still bring language, culture, tradition, temperament, self-interest, ignorance, and plain error to interpretation. That does not prove there is no guidance, but it should make Christians considerably less impressed with their own certainty. Agreement has never been the same thing as truth; disagreement means the work of interpretation remains real.
