All current questions.

If God is good and powerful, why is there cancer, earthquakes, abuse, dementia, war, and children dying? Why do prayers go unanswered? Why are there losses that split your life into before and after?

There isn’t a neat explanation.

The Bible never gives one reason that covers all suffering, and I’d rather say that up front than pretend it does.

Job demands an explanation. God answers him, but not with one (Job 38–42). Herod kills the children of Bethlehem, and Matthew quotes “Rachel weeping for her children” (Matthew 2:16–18). Jesus cries at a grave (John 11:35). He warns with frightening seriousness about harming children (Matthew 18:6) and tells His disciples to let children come to Him (Mark 10:14–16).

The Bible says the world isn’t the way it’s supposed to be.

Genesis 3 describes creation damaged by human rebellion. Romans 8:18–23 says the whole creation is groaning and waiting to be set right.

Human freedom explains some suffering: people choose to hurt each other. Stable natural laws explain some of the rest: a world where choices matter is also a world where earthquakes happen. Suffering sometimes produces courage or compassion, too. None of that explains every particular tragedy, and I wouldn’t try using it on someone in the middle of one.

It puts God on a cross.

Christians believe God went through human suffering Himself in Jesus: betrayal, humiliation, an unjust trial, torture, abandonment, and death.

That doesn’t answer every “why.” It does mean Christianity can’t talk about suffering as though God stayed untouched by it.

It also makes a claim about the future.

Resurrection means death isn’t treated as a normal part of life. 1 Corinthians 15:26 calls it an enemy. Revelation 21:4 promises a future in which God personally wipes away every tear.

Christian hope isn’t that everything happens for a reason we can figure out. It’s that God can bring something out of suffering He never called good, and that He will judge evil, raise the dead, and finally put things right.

But what about right now?

Right now you can be angry. You can ask for help. You can ask God why. The Psalms do all of that, out loud.

So there’s no neat reason for why a child suffers. Christians believe God takes evil seriously, entered human suffering in Jesus, and has promised to end it.

If that is all you have

God, I don’t understand this. Please don’t leave me in it.

The Bible admits this happens. Isaiah 45:15 describes God as hidden. The Psalms complain about it constantly (Psalm 10:1; Psalm 13:1; Psalm 22:1; Psalm 88).

It also claims God has left evidence in creation (Psalm 19:1–4; Romans 1:19–20), and Acts 17:26–27 says people are meant to seek God and find Him.

The main Christian claim is that Jesus is God’s clearest way of showing Himself (John 14:9; Hebrews 1:1–2). The Bible also says more proof wouldn’t automatically change anyone’s mind (Luke 16:31). The promise is “seek and you will find” (Matthew 7:7; Jeremiah 29:13), though it doesn’t say when. I don’t know why it takes some people so long, and I’d be suspicious of anyone who says they do.

The strongest early evidence is the summary Paul says he “received” and passed on in 1 Corinthians 15:3–8: Jesus died, was buried, was raised, and appeared to Peter, the Twelve, more than 500 people, James, and Paul.

Many scholars date that tradition very early—within the first years of the Christian movement. Historians also weigh the empty tomb, the appearances Jesus’ followers claimed, James—who didn’t believe during Jesus’ ministry but later appears as a resurrection witness (John 7:5; 1 Corinthians 15:7), and Paul’s change from persecutor to apostle (Galatians 1:13–16).

All four Gospels name women as the first visitors to the empty tomb (Matthew 28:1–10; Mark 16:1–8; Luke 24:1–10; John 20:1–18).

It’s not lab-proof. The historical evidence can establish what people claimed and how early they claimed it. Whether the resurrection is the best explanation also depends on whether you think a miracle is even possible in the first place.

The Bible gives several answers, and none of them is a formula.

  • Sometimes the answer is no: Paul asked three times for his thorn to be removed and got “my grace is sufficient” (2 Corinthians 12:7–10).
  • Sometimes the answer is to keep asking (Luke 18:1–8).
  • Sometimes we’re asking for the wrong reasons (James 4:2–3).
  • Jesus Himself prayed “take this cup from me” and didn’t get the way out He asked for (Matthew 26:39).
  • Sometimes there’s just silence, and the Psalms say so out loud (Psalm 13; Psalm 88).

Jesus does say God gives good gifts (Matthew 7:7–11), but that isn’t a guarantee you’ll get whatever you ask for. Romans 8:26 says the Spirit prays for us when we don’t have words.

How do I pray when I don’t know what to say? →

It does matter. Culture shapes what feels believable to everyone, Christians included. But how you came to believe something is a separate question from whether it’s true.

The Bible says God made the nations to seek Him (Acts 17:26–27), people are judged by what they were given (Luke 12:48; Romans 2:12–16), Abraham was blessed so “all peoples on earth” would be blessed (Genesis 12:3), and God “wants all people to be saved” (1 Timothy 2:4).

Abraham asks the question Christians still have to ask: will the Judge of all the earth do what is right? (Genesis 18:25).

The Bible doesn’t spell out how this works for every person, so be careful around anyone who says they know.

Jesus is gentler with honest doubters than many Christians are.

  • “I do believe, but help me overcome my unbelief!” got help, not rebuke (Mark 9:24).
  • Thomas demanded evidence and Jesus gave it to him (John 20:24–29).
  • Jude says, “Be merciful to those who doubt” (Jude 22).
  • God promises to be found by those who seek “with all your heart” (Jeremiah 29:13; Luke 11:9–10).
  • Cornelius, an outsider, was received by God before he understood the whole Christian message (Acts 10).

The Bible also warns about people who run from the light on purpose (John 3:19–20). Christians disagree on some of the details, but the honest seeker is never told to give up.

God, suffering & hell

Christians hold several major views, all argued from Scripture.

  • Eternal conscious punishment: Matthew 25:46; Mark 9:48; Revelation 14:11.
  • Conditional immortality: the lost are ultimately destroyed rather than kept alive forever in torment (Matthew 10:28; John 3:16; Romans 6:23; 2 Thessalonians 1:9).
  • Hopeful or universal-restoration readings: some Christians appeal to passages such as 1 Corinthians 15:22–28; Colossians 1:20; and 1 Timothy 2:4, though Christians strongly dispute whether those passages teach universal salvation.

Jesus’ word Gehenna comes from the Valley of Hinnom near Jerusalem and had become language for divine judgment. The images are vivid, but Christians disagree about exactly how literally every image should be read.

Honestly, the Bible says less about this than church tradition does, so I’d be careful about sounding more sure than the text is.

Christians disagree here. Knowing something will happen isn’t the same as causing it, but that doesn’t fully solve the problem.

Scripture stresses that God takes no pleasure in anyone being lost (Ezekiel 33:11; 2 Peter 3:9; 1 Timothy 2:4). Paul wrestles openly with judgment and mercy in Romans 9–11 and ends in awe, not with a tidy explanation (Romans 11:32–36).

Jesus says of Judas, “It would be better for him if he had not been born” (Matthew 26:24), Nobody in the story treats that as a small thing. Calvinists, Arminians, and others answer the larger question differently. They all insist that God is good and judges rightly (Genesis 18:25), but none removes the weight of the question.

The Bible presents redemption as something God wasn’t improvising after things went wrong. Believers are described as chosen in Christ “before the creation of the world” (Ephesians 1:4), and 1 Peter 1:18–20 describes Christ as chosen before creation.

Creation is called “very good” (Genesis 1:31), creation is subjected to frustration “in hope” (Romans 8:20–21), and Paul says grace outran sin: “where sin increased, grace increased all the more” (Romans 5:20).

The Christian claim is that a world with freedom, love, rebellion, rescue, and in the end a restored creation was worth making—and that in Jesus, God didn’t stay safely outside the cost (Philippians 2:5–8). That’s a claim of faith, not a proof.

Christians disagree here too. These texts—Deuteronomy 7:1–2; Deuteronomy 20:16–18; Joshua 6:21; 1 Samuel 15:3—are really hard, and nobody should wave them away.

Interpreters usually point out that ancient war language was often sweeping: Joshua can describe total destruction (Joshua 10:40; Joshua 11:23), while Judges 1 still describes Canaanite populations living in the land. Israel is also explicitly told it was not chosen because it was righteous (Deuteronomy 9:4–5), and similar judgment is threatened against Israel itself (Leviticus 18:24–28; Deuteronomy 28).

Genesis 15:16 portrays judgment as delayed for generations, and Jonah 4:11 shows God’s concern for Israel’s enemies.

Then there is Jesus, who says “love your enemies” (Matthew 5:44–45) and “put your sword back” (Matthew 26:52). Many Christians read the whole Bible through Him. Some of the questions stay open.

How should I think about difficult biblical texts? →

Jesus & the resurrection

They agree on the core: Jesus was crucified and buried, women went to the tomb, the tomb was found empty, and Jesus’ followers said He appeared alive (Matthew 28; Mark 16; Luke 24; John 20–21).

The details vary: who is named at the tomb, how many angelic figures are described, and where particular appearances are emphasized. Some differences can be harmonized more easily than others.

Different accounts of the same event often differ in selection and emphasis. The question is whether the differences overturn the shared core or simply show that the accounts aren’t identical.

God does forgive, but forgiveness is never free for the one doing it. If someone wrecks your car and you say “forget it,” you absorb the cost.

The New Testament uses several pictures for what the cross does:

  • Ransom: Mark 10:45.
  • Sacrifice and atonement: Romans 3:23–26; Hebrews 9–10; Isaiah 53:4–6.
  • Reconciliation: 2 Corinthians 5:18–21.
  • Victory over evil and death: Colossians 2:13–15; Hebrews 2:14–15.

Christians disagree on exactly how those pictures fit together. The core claim is Romans 5:8: while we were still sinners, Christ died for us.

What does it mean to be saved? →

It would be unjust if Jesus were an unrelated third party forced to take the hit. The New Testament says something different: “God was in Christ reconciling the world to himself” (2 Corinthians 5:19).

Jesus also describes His death as voluntary: “No one takes it from me, but I lay it down of my own accord” (John 10:17–18; see also Philippians 2:5–8).

So it’s closer to the judge bearing the cost Himself than to the judge punishing a random innocent person. Romans 3:25–26 says the cross is meant to show God as both just and the one who justifies. Christian traditions frame that in different ways—substitution, victory, restoration, and others—and the objection deserves to be taken seriously.

Christianity doesn’t say Jesus is the Father. It says the Son and the Father are distinct, while still sharing the divine identity Christians mean when they call God one (Matthew 28:19; John 1:1, 14; 10:30; 17:5).

Jesus really became human (Philippians 2:6–8), so His prayer is real too: early-morning prayer (Mark 1:35), “Abba, Father” (Mark 14:36), Gethsemane (Matthew 26:39), and the long prayer in John 17. Hebrews 5:7 says He prayed with cries and tears.

It isn’t acting, and it isn’t Jesus talking to Himself. It’s the Son living a real human life of trusting the Father.

Jesus? →

The verse is Mark 13:32: no one knows the day or hour, “not even the Son, but only the Father.” The Gospels say Jesus grew in wisdom (Luke 2:52), Philippians says He “emptied himself” (Philippians 2:7), and Hebrews says He was tested as we are (Hebrews 4:15).

One traditional Christian answer is that the Son took on real human limits, and some of those limits included knowledge. The same Gospels also present Jesus as knowing things He couldn’t have known by ordinary means (John 1:47–49; John 21:17).

The doctrine tells you the shape of the claim—fully divine and genuinely human—but it doesn’t make the puzzle go away.

The Bible doesn’t say why. The only time the Gospels describe Him writing is in the dirt (John 8:6). He taught out loud and trained witnesses to carry His teaching forward (Matthew 28:19–20; Acts 1:8).

Luke says he drew on what eyewitnesses and earlier witnesses “handed down” (Luke 1:1–4), and John says his Gospel was written so people would believe (John 20:30–31; John 21:24–25).

So Christianity rests on testimony about Jesus, and that means you can’t avoid the historical question of whether that testimony is reliable.

Why trust the Bible? →

Bible, belief & truth

Christians disagree about how to interpret these texts. The Bible doesn’t give the simple, universal ban a modern reader would expect, and that’s uncomfortable.

The law regulates slavery (Exodus 21:2–11; Leviticus 25:44–46), and the New Testament addresses slaves and masters inside an existing slave society (Ephesians 6:5–9; Colossians 3:22–4:1; 1 Peter 2:18).

Other texts pull strongly against slavery: kidnapping someone to enslave them is condemned (Exodus 21:16; 1 Timothy 1:10); runaway slaves aren’t to be returned (Deuteronomy 23:15–16); everyone is made in God’s image (Genesis 1:27); Paul tells Philemon to receive Onesimus as more than a slave—as a brother (Philemon 15–16), and says there is neither slave nor free in Christ (Galatians 3:28).

Both abolitionists and slaveholders quoted Scripture. The honest answer is that biblical themes helped undermine slavery, but the Bible never abolished it in one sentence. That doesn’t make the problem go away.

Christians disagree here. Knowing what someone will do isn’t obviously the same as making them do it, but that doesn’t settle the whole philosophical problem.

The Bible often holds divine purpose and human responsibility together (Acts 2:23; Genesis 50:20). It also commands real choice (Deuteronomy 30:19; Joshua 24:15) and records Jesus grieving, “you were not willing” (Matthew 23:37).

Christians divide over models—Calvinist, Arminian, Molinist, open-theist, and others. None of them is universally accepted. It’s a real puzzle, and it hasn’t been solved.

The usual Christian answer is neither. Goodness comes from God’s own character, so God isn’t making up morality arbitrarily, and there’s no moral standard above Him either.

The Bible describes God as righteous in all His ways (Psalm 145:17; Deuteronomy 32:4), unable to lie (Titus 1:2), and unchanging in character (James 1:17). Micah 6:8 says He has “shown you what is good,” and Hebrews 1:3 and John 14:9 point Christians to Jesus as the clearest picture of God.

The obvious follow-up is: how do we know the character Christians attribute to God is actually good? Christianity’s answer eventually comes back to Jesus, not just to a definition.

Jesus promised guidance into truth (John 14:26; John 16:13), not that every Christian would agree on every interpretation.

Paul says “we know in part” (1 Corinthians 13:9–12), and 2 Peter 3:15–16 says some of Paul’s writing is hard to understand. The early church disagreed too: Paul publicly confronted Peter (Galatians 2:11–14), Paul and Barnabas split over a coworker (Acts 15:36–41), and the council in Acts 15 had to argue its way through a major dispute.

Romans 14 also leaves room for believers to differ on some matters. Disagreement doesn’t prove there’s no guidance, but it should make all of us less sure of ourselves. Test claims instead of assuming (1 John 4:1).

Short answer: Christianity does not prove God the way you prove a geometry theorem. It argues that God makes better sense of reality than the alternatives: why anything exists at all, why the universe is intelligible, why consciousness and moral obligation are real, and why human beings keep reaching beyond survival for meaning.

The strongest objection: human beings are very good at pattern-making, agency-detection, and inventing explanations. Religion could be one more example of that.

What I’m not sure about: no single argument gets you all the way to the God Christians worship. For Christianity, the case eventually has to move from abstract “God” to Jesus.

Short answer: Christianity says God has made Himself known, but not in a way that removes every possibility of doubt. Christians point to creation, conscience, Scripture, Jesus, and personal encounter.

The strongest objection: if God wants relationship with people, the ambiguity feels strange. Why not make His existence as obvious as gravity?

What I’m not sure about: I don’t know why God gives some people what feels like overwhelming evidence and leaves others searching for years. That is part of the hiddenness problem, not a footnote to it. See Why is God so hard to find?

Short answer: Christian worship is not supposed to be feeding a divine ego. It is recognizing what is actually true: that God is God and we are not. Worship includes gratitude, trust, obedience, awe, and love—not merely singing compliments.

The strongest objection: a being who demands worship can sound insecure or authoritarian.

What I’m not sure about: Christians sometimes talk about worship in ways that make God sound needy. The Bible also says God is not served as though He needed anything (Acts 17:24–25).

Short answer: Christianity says God did not create because He was lonely or incomplete. Creation is usually understood as an act of generosity: God gives existence, life, beauty, and the possibility of relationship.

The strongest objection: if God knew creation would include genocide, disease, abuse, and hell, “generosity” starts sounding very complicated.

What I’m not sure about: I can explain what Christians mean by creation as gift. I cannot make the cost of that gift disappear. See If God knew humanity would fall, why create us?

Short answer: love and judgment are not necessarily opposites. If God loves people, Christians argue He must take seriously what destroys people: cruelty, exploitation, lies, abuse, violence, and evil.

The strongest objection: divine judgment can become terrifying once punishment is eternal or once people are judged for unbelief, not just obvious cruelty.

What I’m not sure about: I think Christianity needs both mercy and judgment to make moral sense. The exact nature and duration of final judgment is one place Christians disagree more than they sometimes admit.

Short answer: yes, Jesus’ existence is accepted by the overwhelming majority of historians who study the period. The debate is about what He did and who He was, not whether a Jewish teacher named Jesus was crucified under Pontius Pilate.

The New Testament is itself ancient historical evidence, but it is not the only evidence. Non-Christian references to Jesus or early Christians also appear in Roman and Jewish sources.

The strongest objection: those outside references are brief and later than Jesus’ lifetime. They do not prove the resurrection or Christianity.

Short answer: Jesus rarely gives the modern sentence “I am God,” but the Gospels portray Him doing and claiming things that, in Jewish context, belong to God: forgiving sins, judging the world, receiving worship, claiming unique unity with the Father, and using language tied to God’s identity.

The strongest objection: John’s Gospel is more explicit than the Synoptics, which raises the question of whether the highest claims developed later.

What I’m not sure about: the wording develops across the Gospels. The question is whether the development invents something new or makes explicit what was already there.

Short answer: they could have developed in retelling, but the basic resurrection claim appears very early. Paul’s letters, written before the Gospels, already treat Jesus’ death and resurrection as central and name witnesses (1 Corinthians 15:3–8).

The strongest objection: early belief is not the same thing as a true belief. People can sincerely believe extraordinary things.

What I’m not sure about: “legend” does not explain everything by itself. The harder question is what best explains the combination of early proclamation, claimed appearances, and the transformed movement.

Short answer: grief experiences and visions are real possibilities and should not be waved away. They could explain some individual experiences.

The strongest objection to the hallucination theory: the resurrection tradition includes multiple people, groups, repeated appearances, and a physical emphasis—not just one bereaved person seeing someone they missed.

What I’m not sure about: psychological explanations are difficult to reconstruct two thousand years later. Christians still have to explain why resurrection, rather than “Jesus is spiritually with God,” became the claim.

Short answer: a stolen body could explain an empty tomb, but not by itself the reported appearances or the disciples’ conviction that Jesus had been raised.

The strongest objection: the empty-tomb tradition itself has to be evaluated. If the burial or tomb details are unreliable, there may be no body-theft problem to solve.

What I’m not sure about: no single alternative theory answers every piece of the evidence neatly. That does not automatically prove resurrection, but it is why Christians think the case is cumulative rather than one clever argument.

Short answer: the Gospels are ancient biographies written within living memory of Jesus, using earlier sources, eyewitness tradition, and named people. Their traditional titles are early, but the books themselves do not open with modern bylines.

The strongest objection: decades allow memory to shift, communities to shape stories, and theology to influence retelling.

What I’m not sure about: “anonymous” can be overstated, and so can “eyewitness.” The real work is comparing the texts, their sources, their differences, and what kind of historical claims they are actually making.

Short answer: manuscripts contain copying differences. That is not a secret. Textual criticism exists because scribes sometimes misspelled words, skipped lines, repeated phrases, or added clarifications.

Because we have many manuscripts, scholars can compare them and usually identify the earlier reading with considerable confidence.

The strongest objection: a few variants are meaningful, and some famous passages are absent from the earliest manuscripts.

What I’m not sure about: “the Bible never changed” is too simple. “The text was hopelessly corrupted” is also too simple. The evidence is messier—and much more recoverable—than either slogan.

Short answer: no single person sat down and invented the Bible. The Old Testament canon developed within Judaism. Early Christians received those Scriptures and gradually recognized a collection of apostolic writings as authoritative.

The strongest objection: there were disputes. Some books were accepted earlier than others, and Catholic, Orthodox, and Protestant traditions still differ over parts of the Old Testament.

What I’m not sure about: “the church chose the Bible” and “the Bible dropped fully formed from heaven” are both bad summaries. Canon was a historical process, and Christians interpret that process differently.

Short answer: there are passages that are genuinely difficult to reconcile. Some are ordinary differences in perspective or compression; others are harder.

The strongest objection: Christians sometimes redefine “contradiction” so narrowly that no discrepancy could ever count, then call the problem solved.

What I’m not sure about: I do not think every apparent contradiction is a contradiction. I also do not think every harmonization is convincing. The Bible should be allowed to be as complicated as the text actually is.

Short answer: the disagreement is mainly about Old Testament books often called the Deuterocanon or Apocrypha. Different Christian traditions inherited and evaluated those books differently.

The strongest objection: if God gave a book to guide the church, disagreement about which books belong in it looks awkward.

What I’m not sure about: the differences are real, but the central story of Jesus does not depend on them. Still, pretending every Christian has always had exactly the same table of contents would be historically false.

Short answer: evolution can describe a biological process. It does not by itself answer why there is a universe, why there are laws of nature, or whether God exists.

The strongest objection: evolutionary explanations do remove some arguments that treated biological complexity as requiring direct intervention at every step.

What I’m not sure about: Christians disagree sharply about Genesis and evolution. I do not think accepting evolution requires atheism, and I do not think invoking God is a substitute for doing biology.

Short answer: yes. Many Christians accept evolutionary science while believing God created and sustains the world.

The strongest objection: evolution raises hard questions about Adam and Eve, death before the fall, human uniqueness, and how to read Genesis.

What I’m not sure about: there is no single Christian model everyone accepts. The disagreement is not really “science versus Christianity”; it is often about how particular biblical texts should be interpreted.

Short answer: Christians do not usually mean that miracles are nature malfunctioning. They mean the God who ordinarily sustains nature can act within it in unusual ways.

The strongest objection: extraordinary claims require strong evidence, and miracle stories appear in many religions.

What I’m not sure about: calling something a miracle should come after investigation, not before it. Christianity stakes a lot on one miracle in particular—the resurrection—and that claim deserves more scrutiny than “weird thing happened, therefore God.”

Short answer: science has replaced many bad explanations of how things work. It has not demonstrated that God does not exist.

The strongest objection: as natural explanations expand, “God did it” can look like an ever-shrinking placeholder for ignorance.

What I’m not sure about: I think “God of the gaps” reasoning is weak. Christian belief is supposed to be about why there is a world with regular laws at all, and about what happened in Jesus—not about using God to patch holes in chemistry or physics.

Short answer: religions make genuinely different claims. They cannot all be true in the same sense when they disagree about God, Jesus, salvation, reincarnation, revelation, and what happens after death.

Christianity ultimately stands or falls on Jesus—especially His identity, death, and resurrection.

The strongest objection: most people inherit religion from family and geography, which should make all of us suspicious of conveniently believing what we were handed.

What I’m not sure about: comparing religions fairly takes more than listing differences. Christianity should be tested on its strongest case, and other religions deserve the same courtesy.

Short answer: that sounds generous, but it usually flattens the religions themselves. Buddhism does not simply say what Christianity says in different vocabulary. Islam and Christianity disagree directly about Jesus’ identity and crucifixion.

The strongest objection: maybe no tradition has the whole picture, and each one reflects part of a larger reality.

What I’m not sure about: that possibility cannot just be dismissed. But “all paths are the same” is itself a religious claim—and one the religions generally do not make about themselves.

Short answer: Christians agree that God is just and that salvation is through Christ. They disagree about exactly how those truths apply to someone who never had a meaningful chance to hear the Gospel.

The strongest objection: if explicit belief in Jesus is required, geography and history seem to determine eternal destiny far too much.

What I’m not sure about: Scripture does not give us a chart for every edge case. I am more confident in God’s justice than I am in anybody’s tidy theory of how every unevangelized person is judged.

Short answer: Christianity does not present saving faith as passing a theology exam. Trust matters because salvation is relationship and allegiance: trusting Jesus, not merely agreeing that certain propositions are probably true.

The strongest objection: belief is not fully voluntary. You cannot simply decide to find a claim convincing.

What I’m not sure about: I think God can distinguish stubborn refusal from honest inability to believe. Christians should be careful about pretending we know exactly where that line falls in another person.

Short answer: Christians disagree. Some traditions teach that genuine salvation will finally persevere. Others teach that a believer can later reject Christ and fall away.

Both positions appeal to serious biblical texts.

The strongest objection: whichever side you choose, there are passages that are uncomfortable for it.

What I’m not sure about: the New Testament gives strong assurance to people trusting Christ and strong warnings against abandoning Him. I would rather preserve both than make either one disappear.

Short answer: the Bible does not give a single explicit verse that answers every question here. Many Christians trust that children who die are received by God’s mercy.

The strongest objection: that conclusion often rests on broader beliefs about God’s character rather than a direct statement.

What I’m not sure about: I would not pretend certainty where Scripture is not explicit. I am comfortable saying that God is more just and merciful than we are, not less.

Short answer: because Christians can be cruel, ambitious, tribal, frightened, and hungry for power like everyone else—and religious language can make those impulses worse when it baptizes them.

Some historical accusations are oversimplified, but there is plenty that does not need exaggerating.

The strongest objection: if Christianity is supposed to transform people, centuries of Christian violence are not a side issue.

What I’m not sure about: Christian failure does not by itself tell us whether Jesus is who Christians say He is. But it absolutely tells Christians they have no business acting surprised when people distrust the church.

Short answer: because becoming Christian does not make someone incapable of hypocrisy. Jesus spends a remarkable amount of time attacking religious hypocrisy Himself.

The strongest objection: “Christians are sinners too” can become a cheap excuse. If the Holy Spirit changes people, there should eventually be some observable difference.

What I’m not sure about: I expect Christianity to produce fruit over time, and I think serious, persistent hypocrisy is evidence worth taking seriously—not something to wave away with a slogan.

Short answer: Christianity does not claim Christians begin as better people. It claims God is changing them.

The strongest objection: if the change is real, eventually it should be visible. Claims about transformation are hollow if Christians are just as dishonest, cruel, greedy, or abusive as everyone else.

What I’m not sure about: measuring moral transformation across millions of people is difficult. At the personal level, though, the question is fair: if following Jesus never changes how I treat people, something is wrong.

Short answer: Christianity can absolutely be comforting. That does not tell us whether it is true. A belief can be comforting and true, or comforting and false.

The strongest objection: religious experience is especially vulnerable to confirmation bias because the subject is personal, emotional, and hard to measure.

What I’m not sure about: I do not trust “it feels true” as enough. Feelings belong in the evidence pile, but they should not be the entire pile.
