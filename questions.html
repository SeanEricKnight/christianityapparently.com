Questions worth asking

Questions
worth asking.

You don’t have to stop asking hard questions in order to take Jesus seriously.

Start with the question you actually wonder about. Not the one you think you’re supposed to ask.

Most asked.

If God is good and powerful, why is the world full of cancer, earthquakes, abuse, dementia, war, children dying, prayers that seem unanswered, and losses that permanently divide life into before and after?

There is no tidy explanation.

The Bible never gives one neat reason for every instance of suffering, and I think that is worth saying plainly.

Job demands answers and gets God’s presence instead (Job 38–42). Herod kills the children of Bethlehem, and Matthew quotes “Rachel weeping for her children” (Matthew 2:16–18). Jesus weeps at a grave (John 11:35) and warns with terrifying seriousness against harming little ones (Matthew 18:6; Mark 10:14–16).

The Bible says the world is broken.

Genesis 3 describes creation fractured by human rebellion, and Romans 8:18–23 pictures the whole creation groaning and waiting for restoration.

Human freedom explains some suffering. Stable natural laws help explain why a world capable of meaningful action is also capable of natural disaster. Suffering can sometimes produce courage or compassion. None of those ideas explains every particular tragedy.

The center of Christianity is not God watching suffering from a safe distance.

It puts God on a cross.

Christians believe God entered human suffering in Jesus: betrayal, humiliation, injustice, torture, abandonment, and death.

That does not answer every “why.” It changes the Christian answer to “where is God when it hurts?”

And Christianity makes a future claim.

Resurrection means death is not merely accepted as part of life. It is called an enemy. Revelation 21:4 promises a future in which God personally wipes away every tear.

Christian hope is not that everything happens for an obvious reason we can decode. It is that God can redeem what He does not call good, raise the dead, judge evil, and finally make things right.

But what about right now?

Right now, lament is allowed. Help is allowed. Anger is allowed. Questions are allowed. The Psalms make room for all of it.

So there is no neat reason for why a child suffers. There is a God Christians believe suffers with us, takes evil seriously, and has promised to end it.

If that is all you have

God, I don’t understand this. Please don’t leave me in it.

The Bible admits this happens: “Truly you are a God who hides himself” (Isaiah 45:15). The Psalms complain about it constantly (Psalm 10:1; Psalm 13:1; Psalm 22:1; Psalm 88).

It also claims God has left signs in creation (Psalm 19:1–4; Romans 1:19–20), and that people are meant to “search for him and perhaps reach out for him and find him” (Acts 17:26–27).

The big claim is that God’s clearest self-disclosure is Jesus (John 14:9; Hebrews 1:1–2). The Bible also says more proof would not automatically fix things (Luke 16:31). The promise is “seek and you will find” (Matthew 7:7; Jeremiah 29:13), though it does not say when.

The strongest early evidence is the summary Paul says he “received” and passed on in 1 Corinthians 15:3–8: Jesus died, was buried, was raised, and appeared to Peter, the Twelve, more than 500 people, James, and Paul.

Many scholars date that tradition very early—within the first years of the Christian movement. Other points historians weigh include the empty-tomb tradition, the appearances claimed by Jesus’ followers, the conversion of Jesus’ brother James (John 7:5; 1 Corinthians 15:7), and Paul’s change from persecutor to apostle (Galatians 1:13–16).

The four Gospels also name women as the first visitors to the empty tomb (Matthew 28:1–10; Mark 16:1–8; Luke 24:1–10; John 20:1–18).

It is not lab-proof. The historical evidence can establish what people claimed and how early they claimed it. Whether resurrection is the best explanation also depends on whether a miracle is allowed into the set of possible explanations.

The Bible gives several answers, and none is a formula.

  • Sometimes it is “no”: Paul asked three times for his thorn to be removed and got “my grace is sufficient” (2 Corinthians 12:7–10).
  • Sometimes it is “keep asking” (Luke 18:1–8).
  • Sometimes we ask for the wrong reasons (James 4:2–3).
  • Jesus Himself prayed “take this cup from me” and did not receive the escape He asked for (Matthew 26:39).
  • Sometimes there is just silence, and the Psalms give you permission to say so (Psalm 13; Psalm 88).

Jesus does say God gives good gifts (Matthew 7:7–11), but prayer is relationship, not a vending machine. Romans 8:26 says the Spirit prays for us when we have no words.

How do I pray when I do not know what to say? →

It does matter, and culture shapes what feels believable to everyone, Christians included. But why you believe something is a different question from whether it is true.

The Bible says God made the nations to seek Him (Acts 17:26–27), people are judged by what they were given (Luke 12:48; Romans 2:12–16), Abraham was blessed so “all peoples on earth” would be blessed (Genesis 12:3), and God “wants all people to be saved” (1 Timothy 2:4).

Abraham’s question still matters: “Will not the Judge of all the earth do right?” (Genesis 18:25).

The Bible does not spell out how this works for every person, so people should be careful about claiming to know.

Jesus is gentler with honest doubters than many Christians are.

  • “I believe; help my unbelief!” got help, not rebuke (Mark 9:24).
  • Thomas demanded evidence and Jesus gave it to him (John 20:24–29).
  • Jude says, “Be merciful to those who doubt” (Jude 22).
  • God promises to be found by those who seek “with all your heart” (Jeremiah 29:13; Luke 11:9–10).
  • Cornelius, an outsider, was received by God before he understood the whole Christian message (Acts 10).

The Bible also warns about people who run from the light on purpose (John 3:19–20). Christians disagree on some of the details, but the honest seeker is never told to give up.

God, suffering & hell

Christians hold several major views, all argued from Scripture.

  • Eternal conscious punishment: Matthew 25:46; Mark 9:48; Revelation 14:11.
  • Conditional immortality: the lost are ultimately destroyed rather than kept alive forever in torment (Matthew 10:28; John 3:16; Romans 6:23; 2 Thessalonians 1:9).
  • Hopeful or universal-restoration readings: some Christians appeal to passages such as 1 Corinthians 15:22–28; Colossians 1:20; and 1 Timothy 2:4, though Christians strongly dispute whether those passages teach universal salvation.

Jesus’ word Gehenna comes from the Valley of Hinnom near Jerusalem and had become language for divine judgment. The images are vivid, but Christians disagree about exactly how literally every image should be read.

Honestly, the Bible gives us less detail than tradition often does, so humility is in order.

Christians disagree here. Knowing is not the same as causing, but that does not fully solve the problem.

Scripture stresses that God takes no pleasure in anyone being lost (Ezekiel 33:11; 2 Peter 3:9; 1 Timothy 2:4). Paul wrestles openly with judgment and mercy in Romans 9–11 and ends in awe rather than a tidy explanation (Romans 11:32–36).

Jesus says of Judas, “It would be better for him if he had not been born” (Matthew 26:24), which shows the tragedy is not being minimized. Calvinists, Arminians, and others answer the larger question differently. All insist that God is good and judges rightly (Genesis 18:25), but none removes the weight of the question.

The Bible’s answer is that redemption was not an emergency plan invented after things went wrong. Believers are described as chosen in Christ “before the creation of the world” (Ephesians 1:4), and 1 Peter 1:18–20 describes Christ as chosen before creation.

Creation is called “very good” (Genesis 1:31), creation is subjected to frustration “in hope” (Romans 8:20–21), and Paul says grace outran sin: “where sin increased, grace increased all the more” (Romans 5:20).

The Christian claim is that a world containing freedom, love, rebellion, redemption, and restored creation was worth making—and that in Jesus, God did not remain safely outside the cost (Philippians 2:5–8). That is a claim of faith, not a proof.

Christians disagree here too. These texts—Deuteronomy 7:1–2; Deuteronomy 20:16–18; Joshua 6:21; 1 Samuel 15:3—are really hard, and they should not be waved away.

Things interpreters point out include the use of sweeping ancient-war language: Joshua can describe total destruction (Joshua 10:40; Joshua 11:23), while Judges 1 still describes Canaanite populations living in the land. Israel is also explicitly told it was not chosen because it was righteous (Deuteronomy 9:4–5), and similar judgment is threatened against Israel itself (Leviticus 18:24–28; Deuteronomy 28).

Genesis 15:16 portrays judgment as delayed for generations, and Jonah 4:11 shows God’s concern for Israel’s enemies.

Then there is Jesus, who says “love your enemies” (Matthew 5:44–45) and “put your sword back” (Matthew 26:52). Many Christians read the whole Bible through Him. Some of the questions stay open.

How should I think about difficult biblical texts? →

Jesus & the resurrection

They agree on the core: Jesus was crucified and buried, women went to the tomb, the tomb was found empty, and Jesus’ followers said He appeared alive (Matthew 28; Mark 16; Luke 24; John 20–21).

The details vary: who is named at the tomb, how many angelic figures are described, and where particular appearances are emphasized. Some differences can be harmonized more easily than others.

Different accounts of the same event often differ in selection and emphasis. The question is whether the differences overturn the shared core or simply show that the accounts are not identical.

God does forgive, but forgiveness is never free for the one doing it. If someone wrecks your car and you say “forget it,” you absorb the cost.

The New Testament uses several pictures for what the cross does:

  • Ransom: Mark 10:45.
  • Sacrifice and atonement: Romans 3:23–26; Hebrews 9–10; Isaiah 53:4–6.
  • Reconciliation: 2 Corinthians 5:18–21.
  • Victory over evil and death: Colossians 2:13–15; Hebrews 2:14–15.

Christians disagree on exactly how those pictures fit together. The core claim is Romans 5:8: while we were still sinners, Christ died for us.

What does it mean to be saved? →

It would be unjust if Jesus were an unrelated third party forced to take the hit. The New Testament says something different: “God was in Christ reconciling the world to himself” (2 Corinthians 5:19).

Jesus also describes His death as voluntary: “No one takes it from me, but I lay it down of my own accord” (John 10:17–18; see also Philippians 2:5–8).

So it is closer to the judge bearing the cost Himself than to the judge punishing a random innocent person. Romans 3:25–26 says the cross is meant to show God as both just and the one who justifies. Christian traditions frame that in different ways—substitution, victory, restoration, and others—and the objection deserves to be taken seriously.

Christianity does not say Jesus is the Father. It says the Son and the Father are distinct while sharing the one divine identity (Matthew 28:19; John 1:1, 14; 10:30; 17:5).

Jesus really became human (Philippians 2:6–8), so His prayer is real too: early-morning prayer (Mark 1:35), “Abba, Father” (Mark 14:36), Gethsemane (Matthew 26:39), and the long prayer in John 17. Hebrews 5:7 says He prayed “with fervent cries and tears.”

It is not acting, and it is not Jesus talking to Himself. It is the Son living a real human life of trust and communion with the Father.

Jesus? →

The verse is Mark 13:32: no one knows the day or hour, “not even the Son, but only the Father.” The Gospels say Jesus grew in wisdom (Luke 2:52), Philippians says He “emptied himself” (Philippians 2:7), and Hebrews says He was tested as we are (Hebrews 4:15).

The traditional Christian answer is that the Son took on real human limits, and some of those limits included knowledge. The same Gospels also present Jesus as knowing things He could not know by ordinary means (John 1:47–49; John 21:17).

The doctrine tells you the shape of the claim—fully divine and genuinely human—but it does not make the mystery disappear.

The Bible does not say why. The only time the Gospels describe Him writing is in the dirt (John 8:6). He taught orally and trained witnesses to carry His teaching on (Matthew 28:19–20; Acts 1:8).

Luke says he drew on what eyewitnesses and earlier witnesses “handed down” (Luke 1:1–4), and John says his Gospel was written so people would believe (John 20:30–31; John 21:24–25).

So Christianity rests on testimony about Jesus, and that means the historical question of whether that testimony is reliable cannot be avoided.

Bible, belief & truth

Christians disagree about how to interpret these texts. The Bible does not give the simple universal prohibition a modern reader may expect, and that is uncomfortable.

The law regulates slavery (Exodus 21:2–11; Leviticus 25:44–46), and the New Testament addresses slaves and masters inside an existing slave society (Ephesians 6:5–9; Colossians 3:22–4:1; 1 Peter 2:18).

Other texts pull strongly against slavery: kidnapping someone to enslave them is condemned (Exodus 21:16; 1 Timothy 1:10); runaway slaves are not to be returned (Deuteronomy 23:15–16); everyone is made in God’s image (Genesis 1:27); Paul tells Philemon to receive Onesimus “no longer as a slave, but… a dear brother” (Philemon 15–16), and says there is neither slave nor free in Christ (Galatians 3:28).

Both abolitionists and slaveholders quoted Scripture. The honest answer is that biblical themes helped undermine slavery, but the Bible did not simply abolish the institution in one sentence. That does not make the problem small.

Christians disagree here. Knowing an action is not obviously the same thing as causing it, but that does not resolve the whole philosophical problem.

The Bible often holds divine purpose and human responsibility together (Acts 2:23; Genesis 50:20). It also commands real choice (Deuteronomy 30:19; Joshua 24:15) and records Jesus grieving, “you were not willing” (Matthew 23:37).

Christians divide over models—Calvinist, Arminian, Molinist, open-theist, and others. No single model is universally accepted. It is a real puzzle, not a solved one.

The usual Christian answer is neither: goodness comes from God’s own character. God does not make up morality arbitrarily, and there is no moral standard above God either.

The Bible describes God as righteous in all His ways (Psalm 145:17; Deuteronomy 32:4), unable to lie (Titus 1:2), and unchanging in character (James 1:17). Micah 6:8 says He has “shown you what is good,” and Hebrews 1:3 and John 14:9 point Christians to Jesus as the clearest picture of God.

The follow-up question is fair: how do we know the character attributed to God really is good? Christianity’s answer eventually comes back to Jesus, not merely to a definition.

Jesus promised guidance into truth (John 14:26; John 16:13), not that every Christian would agree on every interpretation.

Paul says “we know in part” (1 Corinthians 13:9–12), and 2 Peter 3:15–16 says some of Paul’s writing is hard to understand. The early church disagreed too: Paul publicly confronted Peter (Galatians 2:11–14), Paul and Barnabas split over a coworker (Acts 15:36–41), and the council in Acts 15 had to argue its way through a major dispute.

Romans 14 also leaves room for believers to differ on some matters. Disagreement does not prove there is no guidance, but it should make all of us more humble about our own certainty. Test claims rather than assuming (1 John 4:1).
