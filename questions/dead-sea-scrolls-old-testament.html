Do the Dead Sea Scrolls prove the Old Testament hasn’t changed?

Short answer: No. They show that much of the Hebrew Bible was copied with remarkable care over about a thousand years, but they also show real variation between ancient copies.

What should we test? Compare the Great Isaiah Scroll (dated to roughly the second century BC) with the Masoretic Text, whose oldest complete copies date to around 1000 AD. The two agree closely in most of Isaiah. Differences are mostly spelling and small wording variants, though some affect sense.

Competing readings: One reading: the scrolls confirm remarkable accuracy in transmission. The Great Isaiah Scroll, copied by a single scribe around 125–100 BC, shares the same basic consonantal text as the Masoretic Text, with a few hundred variants. Another: the scrolls show that several text forms circulated before the Bible was standardized. Scholars describe the Isaiah scroll itself as a revised, updated edition, with modernized spelling, harmonized readings and occasional interpretive changes, while other Qumran manuscripts stay closer to the Masoretic Text. Some scrolls are closer to the Septuagint or the Samaritan Pentateuch. Esther is missing from the finds.

The strongest objection: “Hasn’t changed” can mean three different things: that a faithful copy was passed on, that no variants exist, or that the earliest text equals what the authors wrote. The scrolls support the first, contradict the second, and cannot settle the third because they are copies, not originals.

What remains uncertain: How much textual diversity existed in Judaism of the time, and how any single scroll relates to the community that held it. Scholars disagree on how to classify the texts and who produced them.

Further research: F. J. Gonçalves, “Isaiah Scroll, The,” Anchor Bible Dictionary (1992)
