Questions · Jesus & resurrection

Is the Church of the Holy Sepulchre really Jesus’ tomb?

Short answer: It might be, but nobody can prove it. The tradition goes back to the early fourth century, about 300 years after the burial. The site is plausible archaeologically, but the evidence for the first-century identification is thin.

What should we test? Check what the tradition says: Eusebius reports that around 325 AD Constantine’s agents located the tomb beneath a Roman temple built by Hadrian. Then check what archaeology shows: the area was outside the first-century city wall, and rock-cut tombs of the right period have been found there. In 2016, restorers opened the tomb and found a limestone burial shelf under later marble.

Competing readings: Defenders argue that local Christians would have remembered the location, and that Hadrian’s temple may have been built deliberately over it. Skeptics argue that three centuries is a long gap, that Eusebius gives no reason for identifying this tomb, and that other sites, such as the Garden Tomb, have been proposed, though few scholars support them.

The strongest objection: Findings in 2016 helped date the structures, but a limestone shelf in a rock-cut tomb is consistent with many Jewish burials. Even researchers involved said it is impossible to prove this was Jesus’ tomb.

What remains uncertain: Whether the place was continuously remembered between 30 and 325 AD, and why Eusebius identified this particular tomb. No decisive evidence is known.
