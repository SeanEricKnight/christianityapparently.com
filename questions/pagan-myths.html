Questions · Jesus & resurrection

Was Jesus copied from older pagan myths?

Short answer: I don’t think so, at least not in the way people usually mean. Christianity shares a world with other ancient religions, so some overlap is expected, but the popular claim that Jesus was copied from older dying-and-rising gods falls apart when you look for the actual sources.

What the claim usually gets wrong:

  • Many of the famous parallels aren’t in the ancient texts. The claims that Horus, Mithras or Krishna had a December 25 virgin birth, twelve disciples and a resurrection circulate widely online. As far as I can tell they trace back to modern writers rather than to Egyptian, Persian or Indian sources. For Mithras specifically, no ancient source gives his birthday. The December 25 festival we can document belongs to Sol Invictus, on a mid-fourth-century calendar (Roger Beck is one scholar who points this out), well after the New Testament.
  • “Dying-and-rising god” is a modern category. James Frazer popularized it in The Golden Bough (1890). Jonathan Z. Smith later argued that it forced different figures into one pattern. Many of them die and simply stay dead, or return with the seasons. Tryggve Mettinger (The Riddle of Resurrection, 2001) defended a narrower version of the category. But he treats it as a similarity of type and does not claim it shows historical influence on Christianity.
  • The core Christian claim looks Jewish, not pagan. The earliest summary of it is 1 Corinthians 15:3–8, which Paul says he “received,” so it predates his letter. It claims a historical man was bodily raised and seen by named witnesses. That fits Jewish hope (Daniel 12:2, Ezekiel 37), not Greek thinking, where resurrection was often treated as impossible (Aeschylus, Eumenides 647–648). N. T. Wright argues this at length in The Resurrection of the Son of God (2003).

The strongest objection: this is an old charge, and it isn’t baseless. Justin Martyr (First Apology 21, around AD 150) already pointed out that Christians say things about Jesus that pagans say about the sons of Zeus. Christians also used Greco-Roman titles for Jesus, such as savior, lord and son of God, that were applied to emperors. Later Christian practice did absorb local customs and calendar dates. So “Christianity borrowed language and customs” is true. The real question is whether the central claim, that a crucified Jewish teacher was raised, was borrowed. I haven’t seen a specific pre-Christian myth that matches it.

What I’m not sure about: where the Christmas date comes from is disputed. Some scholars argue from an early calculation of Jesus’ conception, others from the Roman sun festival. No one knows the day Jesus was born. I also can’t prove that no pagan idea ever influenced a Christian detail. And even if some motifs did echo older stories, that wouldn’t settle whether the resurrection happened. That depends on the evidence for it.
