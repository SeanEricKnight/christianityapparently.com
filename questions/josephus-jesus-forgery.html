Questions · Jesus & resurrection

Is Josephus’s passage about Jesus a forgery?

Short answer: Partly. Most scholars think the famous passage (Antiquities 18.63–64) was altered by Christian copyists but contains an authentic core. A second passage, mentioning the brother of Jesus called Christ, is widely accepted as genuine.

What should we test? Compare the Greek text, which calls Jesus “the Christ” and reports the resurrection, with how Josephus, a Jew who did not follow Jesus, would write. Origen said Josephus did not accept Jesus as the Christ, yet the passage as we have it sounds like belief. Eusebius quotes it in about 324 AD in nearly its present form.

Competing readings: Many scholars think Josephus wrote a shorter, neutral account that Christians expanded; one reviewer describes the mainstream view as “authentic but corrupt.” A tenth-century Arabic version lacks the clearly Christian lines and may reflect something nearer to the original, though one scholar argues it derives from Eusebius’s text. A minority holds the entire passage was inserted by Eusebius or another Christian. At the other end, T. C. Schmidt’s 2025 Oxford book argues the passage is substantially authentic, using style analysis and the way early Christians read it, and other scholars dispute some of his steps.

The strongest objection: Even if the whole passage were inauthentic, the James passage and Tacitus would still attest that Jesus existed and was executed. Josephus is not the only evidence, and nothing here shows what Jesus did or whether he rose.

What remains uncertain: The exact original wording. Without an early manuscript before Eusebius, any reconstruction is educated inference.

Further research: T. C. Schmidt, Josephus and Jesus (Oxford University Press, 2025; open access PDF), argues for substantial authenticity; Roger Pearse: a review of Schmidt’s book with comments on how it was received
