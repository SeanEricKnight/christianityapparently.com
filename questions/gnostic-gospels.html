Why aren’t the Gnostic gospels in the Bible?

Short answer: because Christians in the second century were already treating Matthew, Mark, Luke and John as the Gospels, and the so-called Gnostic gospels were written later and teach something different about who Jesus is. They weren’t suppressed from the Bible. They were never in it.

What we actually know:

  • The texts are real, and they were found recently. In 1945 a collection of thirteen papyrus books turned up near Nag Hammadi in Egypt. They contain about fifty texts in Coptic, including the Gospel of Thomas. The manuscripts date from about the fourth century. They are translations of earlier Greek writings, some of which are older.
  • The four Gospels were already the standard around AD 180. Irenaeus argued in Against Heresies 3.11.8 that there can be only four Gospels. He wrote against groups that used other texts, including the Valentinians’ “Gospel of Truth” (3.11.9). The Muratorian Fragment lists four Gospels and no others, though scholars argue about whether it comes from the late second century or the fourth. Tatian’s Diatessaron (around AD 170) was a single narrative woven from the same four.
  • No council banned the others. Athanasius’s Easter letter of AD 367 lists the twenty-seven New Testament books we have. Councils later confirmed lists that churches were already using. See Who decided which books belong in the Bible?
  • The Gospel of Thomas is the most debated. It is a collection of sayings with no birth, crucifixion or resurrection story. Scholars date it anywhere from the late first century to the third, and most favor the second. It is first cited by name by Hippolytus, around AD 222–235. Its last saying (114) has Jesus promising to make Mary “male” so she can enter the kingdom, which is hard to read as a champion of women.

The strongest objection: the winners wrote the history. Walter Bauer (Orthodoxy and Heresy in Earliest Christianity, 1934) argued that early Christianity was more varied than later church writers admitted, and Elaine Pagels (The Gnostic Gospels, 1979) brought that argument to a wide audience. Both are right that early Christianity was diverse and that Irenaeus was no neutral referee. Some scholars, such as Michael Williams (Rethinking “Gnosticism”, 1996), also doubt that “Gnosticism” was ever one coherent movement.

What I’m not sure about: diversity does not mean equal claims. The churches that kept the four Gospels did so largely because those books were tied to the apostles and to the Jesus-tradition they already held. The Gnostic texts typically treat the material world as a mistake and Jesus as a revealer of secret knowledge, which is a different story from the one in the Gospels. I think that is a good reason to keep them separate, but it is a judgment, not a laboratory result. Whether Thomas preserves any early, independent sayings of Jesus is a live scholarly argument, and I wouldn’t pretend it is settled either way. Reading the Gnostic texts is worthwhile. Just read them as what they are.
