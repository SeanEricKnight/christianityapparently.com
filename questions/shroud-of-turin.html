Is the Shroud of Turin real?

Short answer: Probably not the burial cloth of Jesus, on the best available evidence. Radiocarbon tests in 1988 dated it to about 1260–1390 AD. Defenders challenge that result, but no alternative has convinced most scientists or historians.

What should we test? The 1988 test: Oxford, Zurich and Arizona each dated a sample from one corner of the cloth and agreed on 1260–1390 AD at 95% confidence (published in Nature). Also test the historical record: the shroud appears reliably in France in the 1350s, and a bishop reportedly wrote around 1390 that an artist had confessed to painting it.

Competing readings: Defenders argue the sample came from a medieval repair, or was contaminated, and some argue the sampling was not representative. Critics reply that a textile conservator who examined the cloth found no sign of reweaving at the sampling site. A 2020 statistical reanalysis in Entropy found differences between the labs and a pattern across the subsamples, possibly from contaminants not fully cleaned away, and concluded the 1988 dating does not meet today’s accuracy requirements. That is a call for retesting, not a new date.

The strongest objection: Not every reanalysis has been decisive. Some statisticians say the three labs’ values are more varied than expected, and a few researchers have proposed other dating methods. Those proposals have not been independently confirmed or broadly accepted.

What remains uncertain: How the image was formed. A medieval artifact would not by itself explain the image, and scientists have not agreed on a mechanism. Not knowing how an image formed is not evidence it is miraculous or authentic.

Further research: Damon et al., Nature (1989): radiocarbon dating of the Shroud (full text may require sign-in); Statistical analysis of the 1988 inter-laboratory comparison, Entropy (2020)
