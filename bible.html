Scripture

Why should I
trust the Bible?

Don’t begin with slogans. Begin with what the Bible actually is.

The Bible is not one book dropped from heaven in finished form. It is a library of texts—history, poetry, prophecy, letters, wisdom, Gospel—written across centuries by different authors in different settings.

So what are Christians claiming?

Christians call Scripture inspired by God. Different Christian traditions explain the mechanics of inspiration somewhat differently, but historic Christianity does not require pretending the human authors disappeared. Their vocabulary, style, historical setting, purposes, and personalities are visible everywhere.

Can we know what the text originally said?

The biblical manuscripts were copied by hand, so there are textual variants. That fact is not hidden; textual criticism is the discipline scholars use to compare manuscripts and identify the earliest recoverable wording.

Some variants are genuinely interesting. Most are minor. The existence of variants is a reason to examine the manuscript evidence, not a reason by itself to conclude the text is unknowable.

What about the Gospels?

They are ancient biographies centered on Jesus. Asking whether they are trustworthy involves ordinary historical questions: when they were written, what sources they used, how close they are to the events, where they agree, where they differ, and whether their claims fit what we know from other evidence.

Those questions deserve actual investigation rather than either “the Bible says it, therefore done” or “ancient text, therefore myth.”

What about contradictions?

Some alleged contradictions disappear when context or genre is considered. Others involve real differences in detail that need to be examined honestly.

A good answer does not start by deciding in advance that every difficulty must be trivial. Nor does a difficult passage automatically establish that the whole Bible is false.

And the morally disturbing parts?

There are texts about violence, slavery, judgment, sex, war, and the treatment of outsiders that should bother a serious reader enough to ask what is going on.

They need historical context, literary context, theological context, and sometimes the humility to admit that an answer remains difficult.

This page is a starting point, not an attempt to settle centuries of biblical scholarship in six paragraphs. That would be suspiciously efficient.

Where should I start?

If your real question is Christianity, start with one of the Gospels and read Jesus in context. Mark is short. John is reflective. Luke pays careful attention to outsiders and the overlooked.
