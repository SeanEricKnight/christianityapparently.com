SPECIAL INVESTIGATION

CAN WE TRUST THE TEXTS?

“Copies of copies” is not nonsense. It is a basic fact of ancient transmission. The mistake is pretending it either destroys every historical claim or proves the text was transmitted perfectly.

Copies of copies

We have no original New Testament manuscripts. Scribes copied earlier copies, sometimes correcting them and sometimes introducing new errors. Thousands of variants exist, although many are minor. More manuscripts mean more comparison—not automatic certainty.

Oral tradition

Jesus taught before the Gospels were written. Stories were told and retold in communities, and some earlier traditions were embedded in Paul's letters. Oral memory can be structured and stable in parts while allowing variation in wording and detail. Neither “telephone game” nor “perfect dictation” is an adequate model.

The dating gap

Paul's undisputed letters precede the canonical Gospel narratives. Surviving physical Gospel manuscripts are later than the events described. A copy's date is not necessarily its composition date, but the gap limits what we can claim to know directly.

What a manuscript proves

Comparing manuscripts can reveal additions, omissions, and earlier readings. It cannot independently prove a miracle occurred, that a quoted speech is verbatim, or that God inspired the author. Textual integrity, historical accuracy, and divine inspiration are three different claims.

The verdict: The text is investigable, not untouchable. Some readings are strongly reconstructable; others are genuinely disputed. Historical conclusions require additional evidence. Theology requires additional argument.

THE CASE FILES

TEST THE CLAIMS.

Ordered from transmission and authorship to history, interpretation, and moral problems. This is an editorial sequence, not a measured popularity ranking.

The Bible is copies of copies

The problem. No original New Testament autograph survives. The earliest fragments are commonly dated to the second century, and complete fourth-century codices are much later than composition. Copyists introduced variants, sometimes deliberately.

What the evidence supports. Compare independent manuscripts, ancient translations, and quotations. Textual criticism often recovers an early reading with substantial confidence, but not everywhere. A reconstructed text is not the missing autograph.

Key texts: John 1:18; 1 Thessalonians 2:7 · Research source ↗

The Jesus stories passed through oral tradition

The problem. Jesus probably taught orally, in Aramaic, while our written Gospels survive in Greek. Stories circulated before and alongside written sources. Memory can preserve core patterns while changing details.

What the evidence supports. Oral transmission is not automatically a game of telephone: communities repeat and correct material. It is also not a recording device. We cannot simply assume every quotation is verbatim.

Key texts: Luke 1:1–4; 1 Corinthians 15:3–8

The Gospels are anonymous and written later

The problem. The four Gospels do not identify their authors in the body of the narratives. Traditional names come from early church attribution; composition dates are debated and commonly placed decades after Jesus' death.

What the evidence supports. Anonymity does not nullify historical value, but eyewitness authorship cannot just be asserted. Test literary dependence, geography, chronology, and external testimony.

Key texts: Luke 1:1–4; John 21:24 · Research source ↗

The Gospels disagree about events

The problem. Genealogies differ; resurrection accounts vary in who visits the tomb and what they encounter; sequence and speeches are not identical.

What the evidence supports. Some differences reflect selectivity or literary arrangement; others remain difficult to harmonize. Distinguish normal variation from genuine contradiction rather than declaring all solved.

Key texts: Matthew 1; Luke 3; Matthew 28; Luke 24 · Research source ↗

Mark has a disputed ending

The problem. The earliest surviving complete Greek codices end Mark at 16:8. The familiar verses 9–20 appear in later and other witnesses.

What the evidence supports. The longer ending is generally considered secondary. Mark 16:6 already announces resurrection; the addition supplies appearances and further instructions. Editions should flag the passage.

Key texts: Mark 16:8–20

The woman caught in adultery may be a later insertion

The problem. John 7:53–8:11 is missing from important early witnesses and appears at different locations in some manuscripts.

What the evidence supports. It may preserve an early story about Jesus, but it probably was not originally part of John's Gospel. Whether the event happened is a different question.

Key texts: John 7:53–8:11 · Research source ↗

Some explicit Trinitarian wording was added

The problem. The longer Trinitarian wording at 1 John 5:7–8 in the King James tradition has weak support in the early Greek manuscript record.

What the evidence supports. The passage should not be cited as an original proof text. The Trinity must be argued from other passages and historical theology.

Key texts: 1 John 5:7–8 · Research source ↗

The Bible has different canons

The problem. Protestant, Catholic, Eastern Orthodox, and other traditions do not all include precisely the same Old Testament books.

What the evidence supports. The canon developed over time through usage and dispute. One meeting did not simply manufacture the entire Bible. Explain whose canon is being discussed.

Key texts: Luke 24:44; 2 Timothy 3:16 · Research source ↗

Moses and other traditional authorships are disputed

The problem. Pentateuchal texts show signs of complex composition, and authorship of some prophetic, wisdom, and New Testament letters remains debated.

What the evidence supports. Editorial history does not automatically make a text false; it does challenge simplistic claims of single-person eyewitness authorship.

Key texts: Deuteronomy 34; Isaiah 40–55 · Research source ↗

Genesis and modern science can conflict

The problem. A strictly literal recent-creation reading conflicts with evidence from geology, cosmology, and evolutionary biology.

What the evidence supports. Christians disagree on Genesis' genre. Reinterpretation can be principled or ad hoc; judge it against the text and its ancient literary setting.

Key texts: Genesis 1–3 · Research source ↗

The global flood presents historical questions

The problem. Genesis 6–9 describes catastrophic flooding, but geology does not support a recent flood covering the entire earth as described in a strict global reading.

What the evidence supports. Regional and literary interpretations exist; they solve different problems and should not be sold as direct demonstrations of the narrative's events.

Key texts: Genesis 6–9 · Research source ↗

The Exodus lacks straightforward external confirmation

The problem. Archaeology has not established the large-scale Exodus exactly as narrated, and proposed dates and numbers create difficult historical questions.

What the evidence supports. Absence of confirmation does not show that no Israelite group left Egypt; it does prevent confident claims that the full biblical scale is independently verified.

Key texts: Exodus 1–15; Numbers 1 · Research source ↗

Joshua's conquest is archaeologically contested

The problem. Settlement patterns and destruction dates at places associated with Joshua do not yield a simple one-to-one match with a rapid unified conquest.

What the evidence supports. Some scholars see gradual emergence of Israel from Canaanite populations; others defend more historical elements. The evidence is contested.

Key texts: Joshua 6–11; Judges 1 · Research source ↗

The Bible includes morally disturbing violence

The problem. Texts describe divine commands involving the destruction of populations, including women and children.

What the evidence supports. Ancient war rhetoric may use hyperbole, but this does not make the ethical problem vanish. State honestly whether a proposed defense changes the meaning of the words.

Key texts: Deuteronomy 20; 1 Samuel 15 · Research source ↗

The Bible regulates slavery

The problem. Biblical law and New Testament household texts permit or regulate forms of enslavement without plainly outlawing the institution.

What the evidence supports. Ancient practices varied and were not identical to modern racial slavery, but coercion and ownership remain real moral problems. Historical context is explanation, not vindication.

Key texts: Leviticus 25:44–46; Ephesians 6:5 · Research source ↗

Women receive different treatment in different passages

The problem. Some passages depict women prophesying and leading; others restrict speech and teaching roles.

What the evidence supports. Interpretations differ about local context, household conventions, disputed authorship, and permanent norms. Do not erase either side of the evidence.

Key texts: Romans 16; 1 Corinthians 11; 1 Timothy 2 · Research source ↗

Prophecy fulfillment claims can be stretched

The problem. New Testament writers often read Hebrew Scriptures typologically, while passages had earlier historical settings and meanings.

What the evidence supports. A theological rereading may be coherent within Christianity without being a prediction demonstrable to a neutral historian. Read each passage in its original context.

Key texts: Isaiah 7:14; Matthew 1:22–23; Hosea 11:1 · Research source ↗

Translation changes how arguments sound

The problem. Greek and Hebrew terms have ranges of meaning, and punctuation, textual variants, and translation philosophy affect English renderings.

What the evidence supports. Comparing reputable translations and critical notes improves interpretation; insisting on one English translation can conceal important decisions.

Key texts: Isaiah 7:14; John 1:18 · Research source ↗

Don't protect a conclusion from the evidence.

Read the primary texts. Compare critical scholarship. Follow the evidence, even when it complicates the answer.
